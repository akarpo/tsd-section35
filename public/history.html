State School Aid Act, Section 35 · 2015–16 to 2026–27

Every Section 35 grant Michigan has made

Section 35 of the State School Aid Act is where Michigan's school budget puts its literacy money. Since the 2015–16 budget, the Legislature has written 27 different grants into it. This page describes each one: what it paid for, who could get it, how much it got each year, who was paid, and what Troy School District received.

By year

How much Section 35 money each budget held

For its first eight years Section 35 was mostly two things: literacy coaches at the intermediate school districts and extra reading time in local districts. The large, mostly one-time grants that districts apply for, Section 35j in 2023–24 and Section 35m since 2024–25, now make up much of it.

The early literacy initiative (Sections 35 and 35a)

Section 35a began in the 2015–16 budget as a package of early literacy programs built around Michigan’s third-grade reading goal. Its pieces have changed almost every year; each is described below.

Sec. 352015–16 to 2017–18

Running the early literacy initiative

General-fund money for the Michigan Department of Education’s costs of running the new Section 35a programs.

Section 35 set the goal for everything in 35a: programs “to ensure children are reading at grade level by the end of grade 3,” so that Michigan would be “in the top 10 most improved states in grade 4 reading proficiency by the 2019 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP)” and in the top 10 overall by 2025. Its money paid the department’s implementation costs; in 2015–16, $100,000 of it was set aside to evaluate the parent-education pilots.

The 2017–18 payment report shows $150,000 of it paid to Wexford-Missaukee ISD. The 2018–19 budget (2018 PA 265) repealed Section 35 and moved the goal language into Section 35a.

Sec. 35a(6), then 35a(5)2015–16 to 2024–25

Extra reading time for pupils who screen as behind

Troy’s one regular Section 35 grant: money for extra reading instruction for young pupils whom screening shows need help, split among districts that qualified by their first-grade enrollment.

Districts could add instructional time “before, during, and after regular school hours or as part of a year-round balanced school calendar” for pupils in grades K–3 identified by department-approved screening and diagnostic tools (pre-K to 3 by 2020–21, and pre-K to 5 from 2023–24). To qualify, a district had to show it ran a multi-tiered system of supports, used approved diagnostic tools, taught the five building blocks of reading (phonics, phonemic awareness, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension), and had trained its K–3 teachers to read the diagnostic data.

The money was divided by each qualifying district’s full-time-equivalent first graders; HFA put the 2017–18 rate at $210 per first grader. From 2017–18 a district could spend up to 5% of its grant on teacher training and up to 5% on screening tools, which replaced the separate grants for those. The first year’s amount was cut from $17.5 million to $13.6 million in June 2016. The 2025–26 budget ended the grant: “Eliminates $19.9 million SAF.”

Sec. 35a(5), then 35a(4), now 35a(2)2015–16 to 2026–27

Early literacy coaches at intermediate school districts

Grants to ISDs to employ early literacy coaches who help teachers of young readers. The largest line that recurs every year, from $3 million in 2015–16 to $52.5 million in 2026–27.

Each ISD is funded for a base number of coaches, and the rest is divided by each ISD’s share of the state’s K–3 pupils who qualify for free or reduced-price lunch. In 2015–16 the grant was up to $37,500 per coach and an ISD had to match at least half the cost; by 2023–24 it was up to $125,000 per coach, with two coaches per ISD funded first. Coaches work with teachers of pre-K to grade 5 (K–3 at first).

Oakland Schools, Troy’s ISD, has received a share every year; its payments are in the table.

Sec. 35a(10), now 35a(4)2021–22 to 2026–27

LETRS reading training for teachers

Free professional learning in the science of reading for teachers, through a provider the department approves. It grows to $75 million in 2026–27.

Enrollment was voluntary and first-come, first-served, opened first to pre-K, kindergarten and first-grade teachers, then grades 2 and 3, and from 2022–23 to other elementary teachers and some special-education staff. MDE delivers LETRS (Language Essentials for Teachers of Reading and Spelling) with Lexia Learning and reports having started training more than 12,000 educators.

In 2025–26 the $10 million was paid to Gogebic-Ontonagon ISD. For 2026–27 it rises to $75 million, $50 million of it from the educator fellowship public provider fund, for an ISD chosen by the department.

Sec. 35a(9), 35a(7), 35m(9), now 35o2018–19 to 2026–27

Literacy Essentials training (Gogebic-Ontonagon ISD)

Money to the ISD with the fewest pupils in the state, Gogebic-Ontonagon ISD, to train teachers and principals statewide in the Essential Instructional Practices in Literacy, working with the Michigan Association of Intermediate School Administrators.

The training uses the Essential Instructional Practices in Literacy written by the General Education Leadership Network (GELN). From 2020–21 the money also paid for regional lead literacy coaches and principals’ literacy training, and from 2021–22 HFA describes it as literacy and math essentials. The governor’s 2019 veto struck a separate $300,000 for principals’ literacy training.

It began with $1 million added in December 2018 (2018 PA 586). It moved into Section 35m for 2025–26 ($4 million) and became its own Section 35o for 2026–27. The payment reports don’t list the 2018–19 or 2019–20 money; from 2020–21 they show it paid to Gogebic-Ontonagon ISD.

Dyslexia and multisensory reading (35b, 35c, 35d)

Small grants, $250,000 to $1 million a year, for training in multisensory, structured reading methods such as Orton-Gillingham.

Sec. 35d2020–21 to 2026–27

Orton-Gillingham dyslexia training

Grants to districts and ISDs to buy training from an eligible teacher-training program for children with dyslexia, which HFA calls Orton-Gillingham. The law has the department pay them first-come, first-served.

In 2020–21 the law described a one-on-one tutoring program; from 2021–22, a teacher-training program. MDE reports that in 2024–25 it paid the $1 million straight to the two approved providers, $400,000 to the Michigan Dyslexia Institute and $600,000 to the Institute for Multi-Sensory Education, to train district and ISD educators.

The 2025–26 budget dropped it and repealed the section (2025 PA 15). It returns for 2026–27 as $1 million in grants to districts and ISDs; MDE says the application opens in NexSys in spring 2027, first-come, first-served. (In the 2019–20 budget, the letter 35d was a $500,000 social-emotional learning pilot, which the governor vetoed.)

The large grants to districts (35j, 35m, 35n, 35p)

Since 2023–24 the Legislature has used new Section 35 letters for large, mostly one-time grants awarded by application.

Sec. 35j2023–24

Literacy training, curriculum and supports (the grant Troy missed)

The one-time $140 million grant of 2023–24, paid at an equal $311.08 per pre-K–5 pupil to the 434 districts that applied, half in 2023–24 and half in 2024–25.

Sec. 35m2024–25 to 2026–27

High-quality literacy materials

Money for districts to buy reading curriculum, interventions or teacher training chosen from lists ranked by the state’s Committee for Literacy Achievement.

The committee grades literacy series, professional development and tools by their likelihood of raising pupils’ reading outcomes. Districts apply for one item from the lists, and the department pays what it determines a district needs to put that item in place. MDE reports 561 districts applied for and received 2024–25 money, which was paid in 2025–26.

The 2025–26 line ($70 million) also carried $4 million for Literacy Essentials and $1.6 million for the MiMTSS Technical Assistance Center (paid to Clinton County RESA). For 2026–27 it is $50 million, with applications in December 2026.

Sec. 35n2024–25

READ innovation competition

A $10 million competition among districts for innovative literacy programs grounded in the science of reading.

Districts applied with a plan and a way to measure reading growth; those chosen got startup money, then competed in rounds on reported growth, with $500,000 for the winning district in each category. MDE received 115 applications and advanced 39, with startup money from $21,000 to $187,500. The money can be spent through Sept. 30, 2028.

Five Oakland County districts received startup money: Avondale, Birmingham, Hazel Park, Oak Park and West Bloomfield.

Sec. 35q2026–27

Literacy dashboard

No money. By March 1, 2027, the department, with MiLEAP and the state’s education data center (CEPI), must publish and keep updating a literacy achievement and opportunity dashboard.

It must show, among other things, the share of children with access to free, full-day pre-K, the share of pre-K–5 educators trained in the science of reading, the share of districts using a curriculum funded under Section 35m, and multiyear literacy results on state tests, including grade 3 M-STEP.

Troy School District

What Troy received, year by year

Of all these grants, Troy has received money from three: the extra-reading-time grant every year from 2015–16 to 2024–25, the one-year summer reading program in 2019–20, and the 2024–25 literacy-materials award under Section 35m.

What Troy didn't receive

Troy received nothing from Section 35j, the $140 million grant of 2023–24, which 22 of Oakland County's 28 local districts received. It also received nothing from the READ innovation competition, the innovative community library grants, or the screening and teacher training grants of 2015–16 and 2016–17. The 35j findings.

Help that came through Oakland Schools

The largest recurring Section 35 grant, for literacy coaches, goes to intermediate school districts. Oakland Schools, Troy's ISD, has received a share every year (see the table). Free teacher training paid for by Section 35 (LETRS, Orton-Gillingham) isn't paid to districts, so these records can't show whether Troy staff took part.

How this was put together

Where each number comes from

  • Budget amounts come from the school aid budget act for each year (Public Acts 85 of 2015 through 25 of 2026, and the supplementals that changed Section 35 mid-year). For 2019–20, when vetoes and a December supplemental changed the result, and for 2025–26, they come from the House Fiscal Agency's line-item summary for that year. Each amount is the final one for that year; when the first-passed amount differed, the card shows both.
  • Payments come from every Section 35 line in MDE's statewide State Aid Financial Status Report, final report of each year, 2015–16 through 2025–26. Later adjustments, such as money taken back, are counted toward the year of the grant they adjust.
  • Every amount is checked. A script in the public repository confirms that each figure's quote appears on the cited page of the cited document and that every Section 35 payment line belongs to exactly one grant, and it fails if either check fails.
  • Not covered: federal literacy money, and state literacy money in other sections of the act (for example, the Great Start Readiness preschool program in Section 32d).
