The grant

What Section 35j paid for, and how a district got it

The 2023–24 school aid budget (Public Act 103 of 2023) set aside up to $140 million, one time, “to improve literacy instructional practices by investing in quality, research-based best practices, and professional learning.”

Who could get it

Any district or intermediate school district, but only by applying: “a district or intermediate district must apply for the funding in a form and manner prescribed by the department. Intermediate districts may apply for funding under this section on behalf of their constituent districts.” MDE opened one application window, in its NexSys grant system, from Jan. 26 to Feb. 23, 2024.

How much

An equal amount per pupil in pre-K through grade 5, based on the 2022–23 end-of-year count. With 434 districts applying, that came to $311.08 per pupil, paid half in 2023–24 and half in 2024–25. The money had to fund professional learning for all pre-K–5 teachers, administrators and coaches, and a “comprehensive literacy system.”

The conditions

To be eligible, a district had to do all four of these (Section 35j(4)):

  • “Use a valid screening tool, as determined by the department, to identify gaps in current literacy practices and complete a needs assessment.”
  • “Submit a plan to the department targeting identified focus areas to address gaps in literacy practices.”
  • “Ensure 100% of current pre-K to grade 5 teachers complete selected training.”
  • “Report to the department and post on its website which pre-K to grade 5 literacy curriculum and benchmark assessment the district or intermediate districts utilized.”

The second year's money depended on meeting them: MDE reports that 420 districts “met the requirements and received their remaining funding,” while 14 “opted not to receive Year 2 funding or did not submit the required documentation.”

Why it can't be recovered

The grant's spending period ended Sept. 30, 2025, and the Legislature repealed Section 35j effective Oct. 7, 2025 (Public Act 15 of 2025). MDE: “Since the grant expenditure period ended September 30, 2025, these 14 LEAs are no longer eligible to receive funding.”

Oakland County

22 of Oakland's 28 local districts got a share. Troy didn't.

Because the grant paid the same amount per pupil, each district's total tracks its pre-K–5 enrollment. Troy, with about 5,200 pupils in those grades, would have been the county's third-largest recipient, after Rochester and Walled Lake.

How we estimated Troy's share

For every Oakland recipient, its two-year total divided by $311.08 equals its fall 2022 pre-K–5 enrollment as reported to the federal government (NCES), so that count is a good stand-in for the state's. Troy reported 5,217 pupils in those grades: 5,217 × $311.08 = $1,622,904. Because the $140 million pot was fixed, adding Troy would have lowered everyone's rate slightly, to about $307.52, for about $1,604,300. Either way, about $1.6 million. It assumes Troy would have met the second-year conditions.

The other five

Pontiac, Clarenceville, South Lyon, Oak Park and Clawson also received no 35j money. Statewide, the payment records show 416 districts and academies with a 35j line in 2023–24 (MDE counts 434 recipients; the reports don't explain the difference).

Troy School District

Every Section 35 dollar Troy received

From 2015–16 through 2024–25, Troy's only regular Section 35 money was a small yearly grant for extra reading instruction, Section 35a(5) (35a(6) in its first year). The state ended that grant in 2025–26. Troy also got a one-year summer reading grant in 2019–20. Its one large payment came from Section 35m, a literacy-materials grant, which is paying for a new K–5 reading program. SECTION 35a(5), 35a(6) IN 2015–16

Extra reading time for young pupils who screen as behind

Troy received it for ten years, 2015–16 to 2024–25: $1,670,726 in all. The state ended it in 2025–26.

What the law paid for

The money went “to districts that provide additional instructional time to those pupils in grades pre-K to 5 who have been identified by using department-approved screening and diagnostic tools as needing additional supports and interventions to read at grade level by the end of grade 3.” The extra time could be given “before, during, and after regular school hours or as part of a year-round balanced school calendar.”

It covered grades K–3 at first, pre-K–3 by 2020–21 and pre-K–5 from 2023–24. From 2017–18 a district could spend up to 5% of it on teacher training and up to 5% on screening and diagnostic tests.

How a district got it, and how much

A district had to show MDE that it had done all of these:

  • Run a multi-tiered system of supports: “a core curriculum and classroom interventions available to all pupils”, “targeted group interventions”, and “intense individual interventions”
  • Used department-approved diagnostic tools to find the pupils who needed the extra time
  • Taught reading through “the 5 fundamental building blocks of reading: phonics, phonemic awareness, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension”
  • Trained its teachers “in diagnostic data interpretation”
  • From 2017–18, complied with Michigan's Read by Grade Three law (MCL 380.1280f)

Every district that qualified got the same amount per first grader: $165 in the first year's law; the House Fiscal Agency put the 2017–18 rate at $210. By Sept. 1 each year, a district reported to MDE which pupils, schools and grades it served.

What Troy's records show

The money went into Troy's General Fund, and no board document we found says what it paid for: no position, program or purchase is tied to it. Troy's proposed 2019–20 budget lists it among the General Fund grants in one line: “Early Literacy – State funds supporting early literacy development.”

What the records do show, in Troy's own presentations, is the system the grant required. In October 2018 Teaching & Learning showed the board Troy's “Elementary Literacy Multi-Tiered System of Support”: a “PA 306 Implementation Flowchart” (PA 306 of 2016 is the Read by Grade Three law) and a 2017–18 calendar for screening every pupil three times a year, grade-level data meetings, Individual Reading Improvement Plans, and progress checks every two to four weeks. The same deck lists the elementary supports Troy used for “Attacking the Gaps”, among them “Targeted Supplemental Support”, “LLI”, “Reading Recovery”, tutoring and the “Summer Success Academy.” The October 2019 update showed grade-level teams (classroom teachers, special education, ELD teachers, literacy specialists and the principal) meeting three times a year to “collaboratively create” each pupil's Individual Reading Improvement Plan, and new daily phonics lessons in kindergarten and first grade.

Those are the grant's own conditions: screening, a tiered system of help, and Read by Grade Three plans. Troy had to have that system to qualify, so the records connect the grant to it, but not to any one thing the money bought.

When the state ended the grant, Troy's January 2026 budget amendment removed the “35(A) – Early Literacy” revenue of $176,655 it had budgeted for 2025–26. The same month, the district's budget-planning slides set a goal to “maximize grant funds to ensure that we are effectively and equitably assigning intervention (literacy, math and ELD) supports,” and listed literacy specialists among the staffing still “in flux.”

SECTION 35a(9), 2019–20 ONLY

A summer reading program for third graders who scored below proficient

One year only. Troy received $25,630.

What the law paid for

“A summer school reading program for grade 3 pupils who did not score at least proficient on the English language arts portion of the Michigan student test of educational progress (M-STEP) and for pupils in grades K to 2 who are not reading at grade level.” A district had to “prioritize” the third graders and could add K–2 pupils “if the program has capacity.”

The Legislature passed $15 million for it in the 2019–20 budget; the governor vetoed it, and a December 2019 supplemental (2019 PA 162) restored $5 million.

How a district got it, and how much

Districts had to apply by Jan. 15, 2020, and MDE was to make awards by March 15, 2020. The $5 million was split among the districts that applied, in proportion to how many of their pupils scored below proficient on the 2019 third-grade M-STEP English language arts test. The payment records show it went to 556 districts and ISDs.

What Troy's records show

Troy applied and was paid $25,630, a share set by its count of third graders below proficient in 2019. The money was for 2019–20, so the program would have run in summer 2020, during the pandemic. The board heard a “Summer Learning” update at its Sept. 1, 2020 workshop, but no presentation was attached to that agenda, and no board document we found mentions this grant or what Troy's summer reading program did with it.

SECTION 35m, FROM 2024–25

Literacy materials chosen from a state-ranked list

Troy received $1,120,928 on its 2024–25 award (paid in 2025–26). Its 2025–26 award of $1,173,876 was still unpaid on Nov. 20, 2026.

What the law paid for

The 2024–25 budget set aside $87 million, one time, “for the purposes described in this section, including payments to improve educational outcomes in literacy.” MDE's Committee for Literacy Achievement grades reading curricula, teacher training and other tools “based on the series' likelihood to increase student literacy outcomes” and keeps a ranked list. MDE's summary, handed to Troy's board: “Districts will apply for funding for one literacy item from one of the ranking lists.”

The line was $70 million in 2025–26 and is $50 million in 2026–27, with applications in December 2026.

How a district got it, and how much

A district applies and names its item. The award has two parts: “a base award equal to the amount necessary for the district or intermediate district to implement the chosen early literacy series or other item,” and “a per-pupil award based on the number of pupils in membership” of the district. Higher-ranked items earn more in both. A district must report the literacy materials it uses twice a year, and unspent 2024–25 money carries forward to Sept. 30, 2028.

On the list in Troy's April 2026 board packet, UFLI Foundations shares the seventh rank among Tier 1 curricula and Collaborative Literacy, 3rd Edition, is ranked eighth, the last.

What Troy's records show: from UFLI to Being a Reader

This is the one grant where Troy's board records trace the money from the application to the purchase. It is paying for the first step of Troy's response to Michigan's literacy and dyslexia law (Public Act 146 of 2024): the district told the board that by the start of 2027–28 “we need to ensure that we have high-quality materials aligned to structured literacy” (video, 1:06:25).

  1. June 3, 2025: the board hears about 35mAt a board workshop on PA 146, the director of Michigan's MTSS Technical Assistance Center told the board that MDE had released the three 35m ranking lists the day before (video, 1:02:51). The superintendent described a review of about a year and a half, starting when MDE published its approved curricula in January 2026.
  2. 2025: Troy applies, naming UFLIMDE's list of 2024–25 grantees records Troy's choice as “UFLI Foundations, 1st Edition” and its award as $1,120,928, which the state paid in 2025–26. Troy's Director of Teaching & Learning later described that choice: “at the start of the year, we indicated our interest in [35m] funds. We did not at that time have the voices of our teachers at the table” … “[UFLI] is very inexpensive … but we received a very large, you know, more than $1 million allocation. We were not able to spend that all. We were spending closer to $10,000.” (video, 1:01:32)
  3. 2025–26: teachers pick a curriculumTroy's current reading curriculum is not on the state's approved list, the superintendent told the board (video, 0:14:27). A review team of classroom teachers, literacy and ELD specialists, special education teachers and principals heard from 14 curriculum vendors and visited Novi, Birmingham, South Lyon and West Bloomfield. In its review, Collaborative Literacy drew 96% for the comprehensive K–5 curriculum and UFLI 88% for foundational skills.
  4. April 28, 2026: the application is amendedAt a board workshop the Director of Teaching & Learning recommended “that the board allow us to use our [35m] allocation to buy Collaborative Literacy Being a Reader. We would actually be able to buy it at scale and to use that full allocation.” Then: “So we amended our grant application, and it was approved.” (video, 1:03:33) The plan: about 70 K–5 teachers pilot Collaborative Literacy and UFLI in 2026–27, with a recommendation to the board in April 2027.
  5. May 19, 2026: the board approves the purchaseThe board approved the “K-8 Literacy Pilot Purchase” 7–0 on its consent agenda. The May 13 memo behind it spends $1,093,710 of the 35m allocation on “Collaborative Literacy: Being a Reader at scale for all elementary students,” covering “teacher materials, student workbooks, classroom books, and ongoing professional learning” (a Center for the Collaborative Classroom quote dated March 19, 2026). The middle-school pilots ($63,939.09) come from general funds; the K–2 UFLI pilots (about $10,000) were below the board-approval threshold.
  6. Nov. 20, 2026: the second award is still unpaidMDE recommended $1,173,876 for Troy for 2025–26, again recorded as “UFLI Foundations, 1st Edition.” On the state's Nov. 20, 2026 payment, other Oakland districts received theirs (Birmingham: $720,411), but Troy's report has no 35m line. MDE pays 35m after a district's budget in NexSys is approved, so the most likely explanation is a pending budget. We don't know, and the records don't say which award the April amendment changed.

This week

The $1 million Orton-Gillingham grant: small, statewide and first-come

Section 35d of the 2026–27 school aid budget (Public Act 25 of 2026) provides $1,000,000, for 2026–27 only, for grants to districts and intermediate districts to buy dyslexia teacher training. MDE's literacy update of Nov. 17, 2026 told districts how it will work:

“The 26-27 Section 35d Dyslexia Orton Gillingham Program Grant application will open for districts in NexSys in spring 2027. Districts will apply on a first-come, first-served basis until all funds are allocated.”MDE Literacy Unit K-12 Updates, November 2026 (sent Nov. 17, 2026)

What to know

  • MDE names two approved providers: the Michigan Dyslexia Institute (MDI) and the Institute for Multi-Sensory Education (IMSE).
  • Districts pick a provider, upload a quote, and say how many staff to train.
  • $1 million is for the whole state, so early applicants get it.
  • It isn't new money for a new idea: in 2023–24 and 2024–25 the same $1 million went straight to the providers ($400,000 to MDI, $600,000 to IMSE) to train district staff free. It lapsed in 2025–26 and returns this year as grants to districts.

Why it matters for Troy now

  • Michigan's dyslexia law (Public Act 146 of 2024) requires districts, by Aug. 1, 2027, to update their reading screener to include a universal screening assessment.
  • From the 2027–28 school year, every K–3 pupil must be screened “for characteristics of dyslexia,” and staff who teach reading must have had professional learning on dyslexia.
  • Troy's board heard a “PA 146 Legislative Overview” on June 3, 2025.

Sources: 2026 PA 25, Sec. 35d; MDE Section 35d grant page; MDE GovDelivery bulletin of Nov. 17, 2026; MDE report to the Legislature on Section 35 grants, Dec. 23, 2025; 2024 PA 146, subsections (6), (7) and (15).

What to ask

Questions for Troy School District

In Troy, literacy grants run through the Teaching & Learning office and state aid through the business office (as in the May 2026 35m memo). These questions are for the district's administration.

  1. Did Troy apply for Section 35j during the Jan. 26 – Feb. 23, 2024 window? If not, who decided, and why?
  2. If Troy did apply, why do the state's payment records show no 35j money for 2023–24 or 2024–25?
  3. Which conditions were the obstacle, if any: the screening and needs assessment, the plan, training for 100% of pre-K–5 teachers, or posting the curriculum and benchmark assessment?
  4. Why hasn't Troy's 2025–26 35m award ($1,173,876) been paid, when other Oakland districts' awards were paid on Nov. 20, 2026? Is the NexSys budget still pending?
  5. Which 35m award did Troy amend, and what will the other one buy? The district told the board on April 28, 2026 that it had amended its 35m application to buy Collaborative Literacy: Being a Reader ($1,093,710), and that the change was approved. MDE's lists record UFLI Foundations for both the 2024–25 award ($1,120,928, paid) and the 2025–26 award ($1,173,876, unpaid). Did the change alter the amount of either award? The 35m story.
  6. Will Troy apply for the $1 million Section 35d grant as soon as it opens in spring 2027, given that it is first-come, first-served?
  7. How is Troy preparing for universal dyslexia screening of every K–3 pupil from the 2027–28 school year?
